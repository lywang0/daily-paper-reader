# Ablation Study of Block Size, Weight Precision, and Scale Precision in NVFP4 Inference for Low-Power Edge-Efficient Neural Networks 

Ovishake Sen, Venkata Nithin Kamineni, Daniel Lobo, Swarup Bhunia, Rickard Ewetz, and Baibhab Chatterjee 

Abstract —Energy-efficient neural-network inference at the edge requires simultaneous reduction of arithmetic cost, mem-ory traffic, and improved hardware performance. This pa-per presents an ablation-focused study of NVFP4 lookup-table (LUT)-based inference for edge-efficient neural networks. The proposed framework combines ultra-low-precision activation rep-resentation, two-level scaling, LUT-based mantissa computation, voltage-scaled storage, and selective error-correcting-code (ECC) protection. The computation decomposes multiplication into sign, exponent, and mantissa paths, where sign evaluation is performed using XOR logic, exponent evaluation uses integer addition, and mantissa multiplication is mapped to compact LUT access. NVFP4 activations are represented using 4-bit FP4 data, an FP8 block scale, and an FP32 tensor scale. A block-size ablation over six edge-efficient models shows that block size B = 16 provides a practical accuracy/storage trade-off, requiring only 4.5078 bits per input for N = 4096 . A weight-precision ablation further shows that FP8 and FP16 weights provide only modest gains over FP4 weights under the same NVFP4 activation path. To isolate the benefit of the NVFP4 data type, this work further compares pure unscaled FP4 activation inference and NVFP4 activation inference with and without retraining. The results show that pure FP4 inference collapses accuracy for most compact models, while NVFP4 without retraining already recovers substantial accuracy by restoring activation dynamic range through FP8 block scaling and FP32 tensor scaling. When combined with retraining, NVFP4 achieves the best accuracy across the eval-uated models, demonstrating the effectiveness of scaling-aware 4-bit inference. Updated hardware analysis across six models shows that NVLUT achieves up to 26 .85 × energy reduction over traditional LUTs under ECC plus voltage scaling and up to 

22 .85 × energy reduction under mixed-voltage operation. The corresponding area results show up to 2.21 × area reduction under ECC plus voltage scaling and up to 1.52 × reduction under mixed-voltage operation. These results demonstrate that NVFP4 two-level scaling and selective reliability protection can enable robust, low-energy edge inference. 

Index Terms —Edge AI, NVFP4, look-up table, LUT-based inference, block-size ablation, weight precision, scale precision, voltage scaling, error correcting codes, low-power, area-efficient, neural accelerators, AI hardware, ML hardware, neural net-works, MobileNet, EfficientNet, ResNet, ShuffleNet. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

# DEEP neural networks are increasingly deployed on edge platforms to support real-time perception and decision making under strict energy, memory, and latency constraints [1], [2]. In these systems, the multiply-accumulate (MAC) datapath is a dominant contributor to energy and area because multiplication requires partial-product generation, accumula-tion trees, and repeated data movement between memory and

compute units [3], [4]. Prior accelerator works such as Eyeriss and Minerva have demonstrated that dataflow optimization, voltage scaling, and cross-layer co-design can significantly im-prove DNN energy efficiency [4], [5]. However, conventional multiplier-centric execution still incurs substantial switch-ing activity and hardware overhead, especially for resource-constrained edge-AI systems. Low-precision inference is an effective approach for re-ducing arithmetic width, storage, and memory traffic. Mixed-precision quantization methods such as HAWQ reduce model cost by assigning precision according to sensitivity [6], while MCUNet demonstrates the importance of algorithm-system co-design for tiny edge platforms [7]. Recently, NVFP4 has emerged as an attractive ultra-low-precision representation because it preserves a floating-point-like dynamic range while using only four activation bits [8], [9]. However, directly combining FP4 activations with conventional multipliers limits the achievable hardware benefit. Moreover, pure unscaled FP4 activation inference can severely degrade accuracy because a fixed FP4 E2M1 codebook cannot adapt to the wide layer-wise and block-wise activation distributions present in compact neural networks. Lookup-table (LUT)-based inference provides an alternative path by replacing runtime multiplications with table access and lightweight control logic. LUTNet showed that neural inference can be mapped effectively to FPGA LUT resources [10], while NN-LUT and other LUT-oriented approaches have explored lookup-based approximations for efficient neu-ral computation [11]–[13]. The LANA framework further demonstrates the benefits of LUT-based neural acceleration for reducing energy and hardware cost [14]. These prior works motivate the use of compact LUT-based arithmetic for NVFP4 inference, where the activation code space is small and the mantissa computation can be expressed using a limited set of precomputed values. Aggressive energy reduction also motivates voltage scaling of SRAM-based LUT storage. Lowering the supply voltage reduces dynamic and leakage energy, but it increases the prob-ability of bit errors in memory arrays [15], [16]. Reliability-aware neural accelerators have studied voltage overscaling, transient faults, and robustness-aware training [17]–[21]. How-ever, in an ultra-low-precision floating-point format, not all bits have equal numerical importance. Sign and exponent faults can catastrophically change polarity and magnitude, whereas mantissa perturbations often behave as small numerical noise. This asymmetric sensitivity motivates selective reliability pro-tection: sign and exponent bits are protected using ECC, while mantissa LUT values are allowed to operate at a lower voltage with controlled error exposure.

A. Two-Level NVFP4 Representation 

The proposed inference path represents each activation tensor using an FP4 data code, an FP8 block scale, and an FP32 tensor scale. This design follows the broader trend of low-precision and mixed-precision inference, where reduced numerical formats are used to lower memory traffic and arithmetic cost while preserving accuracy through scaling or sensitivity-aware precision assignment [6], [8], [9]. For an activation tensor X, the effective reconstructed value is 

Xq ≈ SFP32 tensor · SFP8 block · QFP4 (X), (1) where QFP4 (·) maps the activation to the FP4 E2M1 code-book. The FP32 tensor scale is shared across the layer or tensor, while the FP8 block scale is shared across a local block of B activation values. The block-scale value is computed from a high-percentile statistic and quantized to FP8 E4M3:

B. LUT-Based Multiplication 

NVLUT decomposes each multiplication into sign, expo-nent, and mantissa paths. This is consistent with prior LUT-based inference strategies that replace expensive arithmetic operations with precomputed or approximated table-based computation [10]–[14]. The sign path is evaluated using XOR logic. The exponent path is implemented through integer addi-tion and bias adjustment. The mantissa path, which dominates the hardware cost, is reformulated as a LUT-friendly base-step computation. Let R = W · L denote the precomputed product of the trained weight and tensor-level scale. Let Bm = 4 b2 +2 b1 +b0

represent the mantissa code of the FP8 block scale and let 

N = n0 represent the FP4 mantissa bit. The mantissa product can be expressed as 

P = R · (8 + Bm)(2 + N )16 . (4) This can be rearranged into 

P = Base + Bm · Step , (5) where 

Base = 8 R(2 + N ), Step = R(2 + N ). (6) Because N ∈ {0, 1}, only a limited number of unique base and step values must be stored or generated. Runtime multiplication is therefore replaced with LUT fetches, shifts, multiplexers, and additions. This reduces multiplier logic depth and switching activity while preserving the numerical structure needed by NVFP4 inference. 

C. Selective ECC and Voltage Scaling 

Voltage scaling is applied to LUT storage to reduce memory-access energy. Prior SRAM studies show that aggres-sive supply reduction can significantly reduce energy but also increases bit-error probability [15], [16]. NVLUT applies se-lective ECC to the sign and exponent fields because these fields dominate numerical sensitivity. Mantissa errors are not fully protected; instead, they are treated as tolerable perturbations. This selective strategy preserves most of the accuracy benefit of ECC while avoiding the cost of full-bit protection. A BCH-based ECC flow can be used for the protected bits. BCH codes were originally introduced as powerful binary error-correcting codes [22], [23], and practical decoding can be implemented through syndrome computation, key-equation solving, and Chien-search-based error localization [24]–[26]. On each read, the received word is checked through syndrome computation, the error-locator polynomial is generated, and bit positions are corrected through XOR operations. The key architectural point is that ECC overhead is concentrated on the sign/exponent path, while the mantissa-oriented LUT path remains voltage scalable. III. B. Models and Evaluation Flow 

The evaluation includes six edge-efficient models: ResNet18, MobileNetV3-Large, MobileNetV4-Conv-Small, MobileViT, ShuffleNetV2, and EfficientNet-Lite0. ResNet18 is used as a stable residual-network baseline [28], MobileNetV3 represents a compact mobile CNN family [29], and EfficientNet-Lite0 is included as an edge-oriented efficient model [30]. The models are evaluated on Tiny ImageNet-style 

224 × 224 classification. The experiments start from FP32 baselines and then apply NVFP4 activation inference with two-level scaling. The block-size sweep studies B ∈ { 1, 2, 4, 8, 16 , 32 , 64 , 128 , 256 }. The weight-precision study evaluates FP4, FP8, and FP16 weights under NVFP4 activation constraints at B = 16 . To isolate the impact of the NVFP4 data type, pure unscaled FP4 activation inference is also evaluated with and without retraining. In the pure-FP4 case, activations are directly quantized to FP4 E2M1 before convolution, linear, and common activation-module inputs without FP32 tensor scaling, FP8 block scaling, or EMA-based scale adaptation. The hardware comparison includes four compute organiza-tions: NVLUT, traditional LUT, array multiplier, and Wallace-tree multiplier. The updated hardware figures include six models: MobileNetV3-Large, EfficientNet-Lite0, ResNet18, MobileNetV4, MobileViT, and ShuffleNetV2. Energy and area are compared under two operating assumptions: ECC plus voltage scaling and mixed-voltage operation. In the mixed-voltage case, the mantissa-oriented LUT storage is voltage scaled, while sign, exponent, and critical control paths remain at nominal voltage. IV. B LOCK -S IZE ABLATION 

Table I summarizes the block-size ablation. The FP32 row reports the full-precision baseline. Smaller blocks preserve local dynamic range but increase scale metadata. Larger blocks reduce metadata but weaken local adaptation and increase quantization error. The results show that B = 16 is a practical design point because it uses only 4.5078 bits/input while maintaining competitive accuracy across the evaluated models. The diminishing return beyond B = 16 is important. In-creasing the block size from 1 to 16 reduces the representation cost from 12.0078 to 4.5078 bits/input, corresponding to a 

2.66 × reduction. However, increasing the block size from 16 to 256 reduces the cost only from 4.5078 to 4.0391 bits/input, an additional reduction of approximately 10.4%, while several models experience larger accuracy degradation. Therefore, very large blocks are not attractive unless the system is dominated exclusively by scale-metadata storage. ResNet18 is the most robust model in the block-size sweep, while ShuffleNetV2 and MobileViT show stronger sensitivity to local scaling. EfficientNet-Lite0 remains comparatively sta-ble around B = 16 to B = 64 , indicating that block scaling can preserve accuracy even for efficient architectures when the block size is properly selected. V. W EIGHT -P RECISION ABLATION 

Table II reports the weight-precision ablation under NVFP4 activations at block size 16. The results show that increasing weight precision from FP4 to FP8 or FP16 provides only mod-est recovery in most cases. This indicates that the activation representation and scale quantization dominate much of the accuracy behavior once the network is constrained to NVFP4 activations. FP4 weights are therefore a reasonable option when com-pression and energy are the primary targets. FP8 and FP16 weights are more useful for sensitive models or layers, but they do not fully close the FP32 gap. ResNet18 shows the smallest sensitivity to weight precision, while EfficientNet-Lite0 shows the largest drop, suggesting that layer-wise mixed precision may be more effective than uniformly increasing the precision of all weights. VIII. D ISCUSSIONS AND FUTURE WORK 

The results show that NVLUT should be viewed as an algorithm-hardware-reliability co-design rather than a simple multiplier replacement. The FP4 activation code reduces data-path width, the FP8 block scale restores local dynamic range, and the FP32 tensor scale stabilizes layer-level representation. The optimal block size depends on the interaction between activation distributions, model topology, and metadata cost. Block size 16 is a useful default because it captures most of the metadata reduction available from block scaling while pre-serving sufficient local adaptation. The bit-cost improvement from B = 1 to B = 16 is large, but the improvement from 

B = 16 to larger blocks is relatively small. This explains why the best hardware operating point is not necessarily the largest block size. The weight-precision ablation suggests that a uniform high-precision weight policy is not required. Since FP8 and FP16 weights provide only limited recovery over FP4 weights in most cases, practical accelerators can use layer-wise policies: FP4 weights for robust layers, FP8 or FP16 weights for sen-sitive layers, and NVFP4 activations throughout the network. This approach can preserve most of the memory and energy benefit while improving accuracy for sensitive architectures such as EfficientNet-Lite0. The FP4-versus-NVFP4 comparison highlights that the main benefit of NVFP4 is not simply using a 4-bit floating-point ac-tivation code, but using that code together with scale recovery. Pure unscaled FP4 inference collapses accuracy for several compact models because the activation distribution cannot be represented with a single fixed FP4 codebook. NVFP4 directly addresses this problem by combining FP4 activations with FP8 block scales and FP32 tensor scales. The no-retraining results show that NVFP4 scaling alone preserves useful numerical information, while the retraining results show that the network can further adapt to the scaled 4-bit representation. This validates the efficacy of NVFP4 as the numerical format for NVLUT rather than using a direct pure-FP4 activation path. The selective ECC result is also central to system-level efficiency. Full protection of all LUT bits would reduce the benefit of voltage scaling, while no protection would expose the design to catastrophic sign and exponent faults. Selective sign/exponent protection gives a balanced reliability point by correcting the dominant numerical fault modes while leaving the mantissa path available for low-voltage operation. The updated hardware figures further show that this reliability strategy remains effective when the model set is expanded from three to six edge-efficient networks. Future work should include end-to-end accelerator imple-mentation with LDO efficiency and latency modeling. The precision policy should also be extended to layer-wise mixed 6

precision, where sensitive layers can use higher weight pre-cision or smaller block sizes while robust layers use more aggressive compression. Finally, the evaluation should be expanded to detection, segmentation, language, and sensor-inference workloads to verify whether the same block-size and selective-ECC conclusions generalize across edge-AI tasks. IX. C ONCLUSION 

This paper presented an ablation study of block size, weight precision, scale precision, and retraining sensitivity in NVFP4 LUT-based edge inference. The proposed approach decomposes multiplication into sign, exponent, and mantissa paths, maps the dominant mantissa operation to LUT-based computation, and applies voltage scaling to reduce memory-access energy. The block-size ablation shows that B = 16 

provides a strong accuracy/storage trade-off, requiring only 4.5078 bits/input for N = 4096 . The weight-precision abla-tion shows that FP8 and FP16 weights provide only modest gains over FP4 weights, indicating that aggressive weight compression remains viable. The consolidated FP4-versus-NVFP4 comparison shows that the benefit of NVFP4 comes from both the data type itself and retraining. NVFP4 without retraining already recovers significant accuracy over pure unscaled FP4 by restoring activation dynamic range through FP8 block scaling and FP32 tensor scaling. When combined with retraining, NVFP4 achieves the best accuracy because the model adapts to the scaled 4-bit representation, making it the most effective operating point for NVLUT-based low-power inference. The optimized hardware-performance results across six edge-efficient models demonstrate up to 26 .85 × energy reduction over traditional LUTs with ECC plus voltage scaling and up to 22 .85 × reduction under mixed-voltage operation.
