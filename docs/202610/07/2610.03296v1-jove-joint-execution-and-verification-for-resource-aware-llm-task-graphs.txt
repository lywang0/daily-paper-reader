## ABSTRACT 

Complex reasoning queries can be decomposed into directed acyclic task graphs and distributed across heterogeneous LLMs, reducing latency through parallelism and enabling smaller models to solve complex tasks. In practice, however, the suitability of an LLM for a given subtask may be a priori unknown, and exe-cution alone does not reveal output correctness. We propose JOVE, an online framework that jointly assigns executor LLMs and selects intermediate outputs for paid verification. Verification runs asynchronously and is used to improve fu-ture allocations, so the system must balance spending on execution now against learning for later. We study how to optimize this trade-off under a long-term bud-get and a per-query latency constraint, with stochastic, initially unknown LLM service quality, invocation costs, and execution times. JOVE makes execution and verification decisions by solving a sequence of per-query mixed-integer lin-ear programs. Online learning updates task-dependent estimates of LLM quality based on verification feedback, while an information-gain bonus incorporates the value of learning into allocation decisions. Under a natural set of assumptions, we establish sublinear quality-learning regret for JOVE. Across four reasoning bench-marks, JOVE achieves competitive accuracy against standard inference baselines while reducing average cost and latency by at least 3.17 times. Code: link. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Large language models (LLMs) offer different combinations of capability, specialization, and serv-ing cost. This heterogeneity has motivated LLM routing : instead of sending every query to the strongest model, a router might select a less expensive model when that is likely to suffice (Chen et al., 2023; Ong et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025). Model selection is of particular interest for complex reasoning queries, which often benefit from an agentic workflow that decomposes the original prob-lem into smaller, interdependent subtasks. A natural abstraction is graph-based inference : a planner decomposes a query into a directed acyclic graph (DAG) whose nodes represent subtasks and whose edges indicate dependencies between them. The node outputs are then used to construct the final re-sponse. This structure enables lightweight models to handle individual subtasks and reduces latency through parallel execution of independent branches. Prior work spans three directions. (1) Graph-structured decoding organizes intermediate reasoning or exposes parallelism, typically with a fixed LLM (Ning et al., 2024; Jin et al., 2025a). (2) LLM routing selects models per query or through sequential interactions (Chen et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2025). (3) Graph-aware LLM systems coordinate different model calls within task graphs (Kim et al., 2023; Dong et al., 2026a). Existing routing and graph-aware allocation methods typically learn from offline data or assume free model-quality feedback after each call. However, in prac-tice, obtaining such feedback may require a judge model or human evaluator at an extra cost. In this paper, we therefore ask: given a query and its generated task graph, how should a resource-constrained system jointly select executor LLMs and decide which node outputs to verify, balancing current-query performance against paid verification that improves future allocations? 

Challenges and our design. We study the problem under two practical resource constraints: a long-term average API-cost budget shared across queries, which offers the flexibility to absorb cost fluctuations over rounds, and delivery deadlines set per query according to its urgency and difficulty. JOVE jointly assigns executors and selects outputs for verification, subject to a long-term execution/verification budget and latency constraints. Verification proceeds asynchronously without delaying the current response, improving model-quality estimates. latency while satisfying both constraints. The budget limit couples decisions across queries: spend-ing more on difficult queries requires saving on others. The deadline couples decisions within each task graph: choosing a slower executor at one node can restrict feasible choices downstream, even when it is locally favorable in quality and cost. (2) The second challenge is obtaining sufficiently granular model-quality feedback without wasting resources by verifying every output. A model call reveals its cost and latency but not output correctness, and the final response feedback may be too coarse to attribute errors to individual node-model assignments. We therefore introduce selective node-level verification to provide feedback on individual outputs at an extra cost. Each node con-tains a narrowly scoped subtask and an explicit success criterion. After a selected node completes, its subtask, outputs, and criterion are sent to a fixed verifier (a human evaluator or a judge model), which returns a binary correctness label. This label updates the estimated quality of the selected model for similar subtasks, informing future allocations. Verification is not required to construct the current response and can proceed asynchronously after node completion. We therefore assume that it does not delay response delivery, although its cost counts toward the long-term query cost. In this paper, we propose JOVE , Joint Optimization of Verification and Execution, a framework that jointly selects executor LLMs and outputs to verify under system resource constraints (Figure 1). We formulate the problem as an online constrained optimization problem that maximizes average node correctness subject to a long-term average API-cost budget and a probabilistic latency limit for each query. JOVE makes allocation decisions by solving per-query mixed-integer linear programs (MILPs), using online estimates of model quality, cost, and latency, with an information-gain bonus that values verification feedback for future allocations. Our main contributions are: • Joint execution–verification formulation. We formulate task-graph inference as an online prob-lem of jointly choosing executor LLMs and which outputs to verify, with initially unknown model quality, cost, and latency, under a long-term cost budget and a per-query latency chance constraint. • Verification-aware online allocation. We develop JOVE, a per-query MILP policy combining online quality estimates with an information-gain bonus for verification. We prove sublinear quality-learning regret when verification is frequent enough to learn but does not crowd out execution. • Empirical evaluation. Across multiple reasoning datasets, JOVE achieves average accuracy com-parable to the strongest baseline while achieving 3.7–16.3 × lower cost and 8.3–17.0 × lower latency. Ablation studies examine the effects of different verification weights and resource constraints. 2Preprint 

## 2 RELATED WORK 

Graph-structured inference. Graph structure can improve reasoning or expose opportunities for parallel execution. Tree-of-Thoughts and Graph-of-Thoughts organize and search over interme-diate reasoning states (Yao et al., 2023; Besta et al., 2024). Skeleton-of-Thought, PASTA, and Plato instead exploit independence or explicit dependencies to generate parts of a response con-currently (Ning et al., 2024; Jin et al., 2025b;a). HuggingGPT, LLMCompiler, and S-DAG extend workflow planning to collections of different models, tools, and subagents (Shen et al., 2023; Kim et al., 2023; Dong et al., 2026b). Dyserve further includes verifiers to gate model selection using static offline profiles (Qian et al., 2026). JOVE takes a planner-generated DAG as input and learns online to optimize executor assignments and which outputs to verify under resource constraints. 

Resource-aware LLM routing and paid feedback. LLM routers and cascades improve quality-cost trade-offs by selecting models based on predicted quality, difficulty, or preference (Chen et al., 2023; Ding et al., 2024; Ong et al., 2025; Dekoninck et al., 2024; Feng et al., 2025). Recent online and resource-aware methods further consider feedback, budgets, or latency in their problems (Wang et al., 2025; Panda et al., 2025; Li et al., 2026; Patel et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2025; Atalar et al., 2025), mostly assuming that quality feedback is freely available after execution. AutoMix verifies an initial output before routing, incurring verification cost and delay on every query rather than verifying selectively (Aggarwal et al., 2024). HybridFlow extends routing to task graphs, training its policy on offline data with subsequent online calibration, but restricts each routing decision to either an edge or a cloud model (Dong et al., 2026a). JOVE instead selectively verifies node outputs and learns an executor-allocation policy online. This selective-feedback setting relates to costly-observation bandits, which separate action selection from paying to observe its reward (Tucker et al., 2023; Schur et al., 2026). JOVE extends this perspective to DAGs by jointly selecting executors and outputs to verify under a shared execution-verification budget and per-query latency constraints.

## 4 EXPERIMENTS 

Datasets and metrics. We evaluate JOVE on four reasoning benchmarks: Bamboogle (Press et al., 2023) (multi-hop QA), MMLU-Pro (Wang et al., 2024) (multidisciplinary reasoning), GPQA (Rein et al., 2023) (science), and LiveBench-Reasoning (White et al., 2025) (structured reasoning). We repeat each experiment over three random orderings of the online query sequence, and measure final answer accuracy, end-to-end response latency, and total API cost per query. 

Experimental settings. We use Gemini-2.5-Flash-Lite as the planner and Qwen3-235B-A22B-2507 as the verifier. The executor pool includes eight models of different sizes (detailed in Ap-pendix A.4), accessed through the OpenRouter platform. Unless otherwise specified, we set the verification weight to kv = 5 .0, exploration coefficient to βt = 0 .25 , regularization parameter to 

λreg = 1 , budget-update step size to αλ = 100 , and latency violation tolerance to δt = 0 .1. We use Qwen3-32B as the reference model for accuracy, cost, and latency comparisons. Based on its average cost and latency on Bamboogle, we set the API-cost budget to γ = 2 .0 × 10 −4 USD and the deadline to μt = 16 s. We choose Bamboogle because it yields the lowest cost and latency across the four datasets, resulting in stricter resource targets than calibration across all datasets. 

Baselines. We compare against two groups of methods. (1) Inference-level baselines : Direct invoca-tion, Chain-of-Thought (CoT) (Wei et al., 2022), Skeleton-of-Thought (SoT) (Ning et al., 2024), and Plato (Jin et al., 2025a). We evaluate these methods using the reference model without budget and latency constraints. (2) Resource-aware baselines : WR-Online (Li et al., 2026) routes queries under the cost budget (without latency constraints) by learning cost and reward online as a constrained contextual bandit. Node-level correctness vs. final-answer correctness. JOVE learns entirely from node-level ver-ification without direct feedback on final response quality, maximizing average node correctness (Eq. 2a). Figure 3a shows how this additive surrogate relates to overall accuracy by grouping queries by total verified nodes n ∈ { 1, . . . , 5} and verified-incorrect nodes k. The average final-answer accuracy drops monotonically as k increases for every n, indicating that node-level errors are asso-ciated with lower final-answer accuracy. At fixed k, accuracy is generally higher for larger n. When all five verified nodes are labeled correct (n = 5 , k = 0) , final-answer accuracy reaches 87.3% rather than 100%. This gap may reflect verifier noise or imperfect alignment between node-level success criteria and final-answer correctness. 

Selection of verification weights kv . Figure 3b varies kv on GPQA, reporting final-answer accuracy together with the fraction of executed nodes that are verified, split by whether the verifier judged them correct or incorrect. At kv < 1.5, verification is infrequent and accuracy remains relatively low. A small kv makes the weighted information gain less likely to outweigh the priced verification cost, limiting the feedback available for quality learning. For kv ∈ [2 , 6] , accuracy improves and remains stable, indicating that sufficient feedback is available and performance is relatively insensitive to the precise verification weight. With higher kv > 6, accuracy starts to decline, potentially because verification consumes a growing share of the budget, leaving fewer resources for execution. This regime also shows an increasing fraction of executed nodes that are verified and labeled incorrect. 

Satisfaction of the constraints. In Section 3.1, we introduce a long-term API-cost budget and a per-query latency chance constraint. In practice, JOVE replaces the initially unknown model statistics with online estimates, so the oracle guarantees do not directly extend to the practical policy. Constraint satisfaction then depends on resource-estimation accuracy and budget-price adaptation. Appendix A.4.2 evaluates this behavior empirically: as learning progresses, interval-average costs decrease toward or below the budget, while deadline hit rates improve, reaching at least 96% on all four datasets in the final interval against a 90% target. The appendix provides additional results on the effect of the online learning sequence (Ap-pendix A.4.1), ablations of cost budgets and latency deadlines (Appendix A.4.3), and comparisons of verification strategies (Appendix A.4.4). 

## 5 CONCLUSION 

We introduce JOVE, an online framework that optimizes the execution of complex reasoning queries represented by task graphs. By jointly managing LLM routing and selective paid verification via per-query MILP, JOVE balances immediate accuracy with feedback acquisition for future allocation decisions. We establish sublinear quality-learning regret under the stated assumptions and demon-strate effective budget and deadline control empirically. JOVE achieves competitive accuracy while substantially reducing cost and latency relative to baselines, providing a promising foundation for deploying efficient, multi-step LLM systems in resource-constrained environments. 9Preprint

Theorem 3.5 is intended as an existence result showing that a nonempty range of verification weights ( kv ) yields sublinear quality-learning regret under the stated assump-tions, rather than as a practical tuning rule. A.3.3 EMBEDDING MODEL 

We use a frozen text embedding as the feature map for the LinUCB head. Specifically, to con-struct ϕit(a), we encode the node’s description, descriptions of required node outputs, and LLM API description with Sentence-Transformers all-MiniLM-L6-v2 (Reimers & Gurevych, 2019), which produces a 384-dimensional unit-normalized feature vector. We choose this model because it is small ( ∼22M parameters) and runs fast even on CPU, reducing the reserved time for executor al-location. Larger modern embedding models (e.g., the Qwen3 Embedding family, such as the 0.6B or 4B variants with up to 1024- or 2560-dimensional outputs) may improve retrieval-oriented em-bedding quality, but they are substantially slower and more memory-intensive to evaluate for every task–model pair, increase the LinUCB feature dimension (and thus the cost of maintaining and in-verting the covariance matrix). Moreover, Theorem 3.5 gives a larger sufficient horizon and a looser regret bound as d increases, suggesting potentially slower learning. JOVE uses a dynamic Lagrange multiplier λt to respond to budget overspending. When cost exceeds the target, λt rises and makes expensive LLMs less attractive in subsequent decisions. The overall average costs in Table 1 slightly exceed γ = 2 × 10 −4 USD per query because JOVE spends more in early rounds, when λt is still small. Table 4 shows how this behavior changes over time: interval average costs decline as JOVE adjusts its selections. These finite-horizon results illustrate JOVE’s budget-control behavior, complementing the asymptotic budget guarantee established for the oracle policy in Proposition A.3. In early rounds, sparse call histories can cause JOVE to underestimate costs or high-percentile laten-cies. Cost can also exceed the target while the multiplier, initialized at λ1 = 0 , adjusts to observed spending. As more calls are observed, the estimates improve: in our experiments, interval-average costs approach the budget, and aggregate deadline hit rates exceed the 90% target in the final inter-val. These results provide empirical support for JOVE’s deadline control as its estimates improve; establishing a conditional guarantee for every query under estimated quantiles remains future work. The paper’s focus is the reduction of the graph-level latency requirement to linear MILP constraints and the joint allocation of execution and paid verification. The resource estimators are modular and can be replaced by offline-trained or more advanced predictors. For example, offline profiling could provide better estimates at the start of learning. 

Feasibility in the analysis and implementation. Our analysis characterizes resource feasibility and quality learning through complementary oracle and online settings. For the oracle budget ar-gument in Proposition A.3, we require a stationary policy that selects allocations in Ft(dt) while keeping expected total cost, including planning, execution, and verification, at most γ. This con-dition places the comparison policy within the same conservative node-quantile constraints used by the oracle MILP, ensuring that it is available to the optimizer. For online quality learning, As-sumption 3.4 requires Ft(ˆdt) to be nonempty at each round and the online MILP to be solved to 26 Preprint optimality. Giving the comparator the same resource estimates and budget prices as JOVE allows us to isolate the contribution of quality learning. We acknowledge that feasibility under estimated resource coefficients alone does not establish the true latency chance constraint or long-term budget feasibility; extending the oracle resource guarantees to the implemented policy requires additional control of estimation error. In particular, the latency argument in Appendix A.2.1 applies whenever the online MILP is feasible and its estimated node durations upper-bound the corresponding true conditional latency quantiles at the assigned risk levels. We complement these analytical results with empirical measurements of budget use and deadline hit rates, providing evidence of resource control in the evaluated workloads and motivating further analysis of online resource estimation.
