## Abstract 

Compact edge system-on-chip (SoC) platforms increasingly run sustained LLM inference under thermal constraints, while their CPU, GPU, and RAM share a cooling path. Prefill and decode therefore consume shared, time-varying thermal headroom, yet vendor governors react only near hardware throttling thresholds without knowledge of request state or upcoming work. A control decision that improves cur-rent performance can thus consume headroom too quickly and degrade subsequent service. In this paper, we present 

ThermE , a runtime system that predicts and jointly manages shared thermal headroom for sustained LLM inference on edge SoCs. Its Fast LLM-to-Heat Compiler maps the model, requests, runtime state, and candidate actions to domain heats without executing LLMs. A partial differential equation (PDE)-constrained Headroom Predictor uses ThermPINN for offline thermal identification and a Reduced Headroom Pre-dictor (RHP) for low-overhead online uncertainty-calibrated headroom forecasts. An Uncertainty-Aware Action Sched-uler then selects actions that balance serving quality and future headroom. We implement ThermE atop vLLM and evaluate it across four LLM inference workloads. The results show that ThermE reduces TTFT and TPOT by 40.55% and 12.17%, respectively, relative to vLLM. It achieves a 5.70% SLO violation rate, compared with 12.30% for the strongest baseline, while its predictor obtains a 1.94 ◦C MAE with 11.50 ms overhead. 

## 1 Introduction 

Large language model inference is moving onto compact edge system-on-chip (SoC) platforms deployed in thermally constrained environments, such as satellites and industrial robots. More broadly, global warming and increasingly fre-quent heat waves expose otherwise conventional edge com-puters in roadside cameras, robots, drones, etc to high tem-peratures [6, 27]. For example, edge computers on autonomous vehicles are often placed in compact trunks, where solar load-ing can raise internal temperatures well above ambient, i.e, 61 ◦C to 80 ◦C in direct sunlight [3], as shown in Fig. 1. These conditions raise the device’s ambient temperature and re-duce thermal headroom , which is defined as the remaining 

Figure 1. A thermally exposed edge platform in a solar-heated truck reduces the headroom and degrades requests. temperature margin before thermal protection constrains execution. Meanwhile, semiconductor scaling concentrates increas-ingly demanding LLM computation within compact edge SoC modules, where sustained workloads continuously generate heat. Unlike server platforms, where CPUs, GPUs, and RAM are discrete devices with largely independent power and thermal controls, compact edge SoCs couple these domains through the thermal stack shown in Fig. 2. Heat crosses their thermal interface materials before converging at a shared heat spreader, heat sink, and fan-assisted cooling path. Heat generated by one domain therefore reduces the headroom available to others. Because the shared cooling path dissi-pates heat more slowly than inference workloads change, this shared headroom varies over time. Deployment condi-tions determine its initial value, while workload and cooling conditions determine its subsequent availability. Hardware thermal protection reacts as shared headroom narrows, exposing two limitations for sustained LLM infer-ence on thermally constrained edge SoCs. First, vendor gover-nors are reactive and application-independent. They activate protection from measured temperature, current, or power, instead of the LLM phase, request state, remaining tokens, or upcoming work. As temperatures approach throttling thresholds, the platform would reduce frequencies by up to 50% [19], degrading sustained service. Second, existing infer-ence controls lack visibility into their effects on future ther-mal headroom. Fixed DVFS settings, power caps, batching, arXiv:2610.00267v1 [eess.SY] 24 Sep 2026 Lu et al.

A performance-improving control decision may consume shared headroom too quickly and trigger lower frequencies later. To understand these limitations, we conduct three motiva-tional experiments that reveal three properties of sustained LLM inference under thermal constraints on edge SoCs. First, built-in thermal throttling reacts only after thermal head-room has been largely consumed and then degrades serving quality through staged frequency reductions. Second, the CPU, GPU, and SoC thermal zones heat and cool together through the shared package. Third, hardware profiles with different CPU/GPU frequencies trade off time to first token (TTFT), time per output token (TPOT), and headroom con-sumption. Together, these observations show that thermal headroom is a shared, time-varying resource that must be managed proactively according to serving demand and ther-mal state. These observations expose three critical challenges. First, the heat generated by the upcoming workload must be es-timated before execution for each candidate control action. Second, current temperatures alone do not reveal how the heat generated by each candidate action will affect future headroom. Heat propagates across coupled domains before dissipating through the shared cooling path, and this pro-cess depends on retained heat from recent activity, ambient temperature, and cooling state. Third, preserving headroom competes with serving performance. Hardware frequencies and scheduled work jointly determine latency and how much thermal headroom remains for later requests. The runtime must select actions that preserve serving quality under ther-mal constraints while accounting for uncertain future tem-peratures and the risk of hardware throttling. In this paper, we present ThermE , a runtime system that predictively manages shared thermal headroom for sustained LLM inference on thermally constrained edge SoCs. The scheduler applies this action and updates its decision after the next observation. We implemented ThermE atop vLLM on an Nvidia Jetson AGX Orin. Under the Stress workload at the 65 ◦C ambient setpoint, ThermE reduced TTFT, TPOT, and p95 end-to-end latency by 40.55%, 12.17%, and 13.01%, respectively, relative to vLLM, and achieved a 5.70% SLO violation rate compared with 12.30% for the strongest baseline. The headroom pre-diction obtained a 1.94 ◦C MAE with 24 .36 ms overhead. In summary, this paper makes the following contributions. 

• We characterize sustained LLM inference under thermal constraints on an edge SoC and show that cross-domain thermal coupling, reactive hardware throttling, and the serving-performance tradeoffs of hardware profiles make thermal headroom a shared, time-varying runtime re-source. 

• We design ThermE to compile candidate workloads into domain heat, combine offline physics-constrained thermal identification with low-overhead online headroom pre-diction, and coordinate hardware profiles and scheduled-token caps under hard temperature constraints. 

• We implement ThermE on an Nvidia Jetson AGX Orin and evaluate its serving quality, thermal behavior, prediction accuracy, sensitivity, component contributions, and run-time overhead across representative LLM workloads and thermal conditions. 

## 2 Background 

2.1 Thermal Characteristics of Edge SoC Platforms 

A system-on-chip (SoC) integrates multiple computing func-tions on a single chip, and similarly, a system-on-package (SoP) integrates multiple chips or SoCs within a compact package. These platforms place CPUs, GPUs, memory con-trollers, and memory within a compact module. Recent infer-ence systems exploit these heterogeneous resources and their tightly integrated memory paths [7, 13, 16, 29]. Their com-pute and memory domains commonly share a heat spreader, heat sink, and fan, which couple their thermal behavior through a common cooling path. Thermal headroom is the remaining temperature margin between the current thermal state and the hardware limit. Ambient temperature and the cooling state determine the initial margin. During execution, dynamic power generates heat, while the package, heat sink, enclosure, and surround-ing air dissipate heat. Because these structures dissipate heat ThermE

LLM inference execution on edge SoCs. vLLM maps request processing, scheduling, model execution, and output processing to CPU, GPU, and shared RAM. more slowly than hardware activity changes, the current headroom reflects both the present workload and heat re-tained from recent activity [24]. 

2.2 LLM Inference Execution on Edges 

LLM inference consists of a prefill phase that processes the input prompt and constructs the key-value cache, followed by a decode phase that generates one token at a time from the model state and growing cache [1, 2, 8, 11, 16, 24]. Service quality depends on both phases, with prefill largely determin-ing TTFT and decode characterized by TPOT and sustained output throughput [2, 8]. vLLM is a widely adopted open-source LLM serving en-gine that combines request scheduling, batching, KV-cache management, and model execution [10], covering half of the text inference services [22]. Fig. 3 shows how vLLM maps this execution onto an edge SoC. Input Processing pre-pares requests, the Scheduler allocates a token budget and forms continuous batches, the Model Executor dispatches kernels, and Output Processing returns generated tokens [28]. The CPU handles request processing, scheduling, and kernel launches. The integrated GPU executes model kernels, while shared LPDDR RAM stores model weights and the KV cache. On Nvidia Jetson, the vLLM platform layer adapts this loop to the ARM CPU and integrated GPU memory architec-ture [20]. The scheduled work and active hardware profile together determine the CPU, GPU, and memory activity of each inference interval. 

2.3 Hardware Thermal Headroom Management 

Edge SoCs expose multiple thermal zones whose sensors track the temperatures of the CPU, GPU, memory, and pack-age. Each zone has configured trip points for progressively stronger protection. Nvidia Jetson platforms, for example, reduce CPU and GPU frequencies as measured temperatures

Firmware and kernel drivers may also adjust voltage, power limits, cooling state, or task placement. If high temperatures persist despite these controls, a thermal zone can eventually reach its critical trip point and trigger device shutdown [19]. These mechanisms manage thermal headroom through a feedback loop. Sensors report the current thermal state, the hardware governor compares each zone against its trip points, and actuators adjust power or cooling. The avail-able platform headroom follows the current zone tempera-tures and their configured limits. This hardware loop oper-ates continuously beneath the inference framework and can change the effective CPU and GPU frequencies as tempera-tures evolve. Section 3 measures how this hardware-managed headroom interacts with sustained LLM inference.

• When and how do the built-in thermal throttling mech-anisms influence LLM inference?

## 4 Design of ThermE 

4.1 Design Overview 

ThermE is a shared-headroom management system for sus-tained LLM inference on shared-cooling edge SoC platforms atop the vLLM serving engine. At each control interval, it selects a control action that pairs a hardware profile with a scheduled-token cap to balance serving performance and heat generation under limited shared thermal headroom. The three inputs in Fig. 7 are the request descriptor , run-time state and telemetry , and model descriptor . They specify request demand, current interval work and operating condi-tions, and model architecture and quantization configuration, respectively. Each action pairs one hardware profile with a scheduled-token cap. A hardware profile specifies settings for the controls exposed by the platform adapter. The cap bounds the work admitted to the next control interval. ThermE combines three coupled components in Fig. 7. 

• Fast LLM-to-Heat Compiler. The compiler maps the model and request descriptors, runtime state, candidate actions to CPU, GPU, RAM heat inputs without executing the LLM. 

• PDE-Constrained Headroom Predictor. ThermPINN identi-fies coupled thermal dynamics offline. Online, the RHP predicts temperatures from telemetry and candidate heat inputs, then computes uncertainty-calibrated per-zone and shared headrooms. 

• Uncertainty-Aware Action Scheduler. Beam search selects hard-safe sequences, balancing service and energy costs with thermal runway during normal operation and the cumulative shared-headroom deficit during headroom-recovery operation. ThermE separates the inference data path from the ther-mal control path. Its asynchronous controller evaluates can-didate action sequences outside the inference critical path.

## 5 Implementation 

Prototype and integration. We implement ThermE as an asynchronous controller atop vLLM [10]. A serving adapter exports the model descriptor, scheduled prefill and decode work, sequence lengths, batch occupancy, KV-cache use, and remaining work without blocking vLLM execution. A plat-form adapter samples thermal zones, utilization, RAM ac-tivity, rail power, ambient temperature, and cooling state, 

Figure 10. Temperature-controlled testbed in electric ther-mostatic chamber for Nvidia Jetson AGX Orin experiments. represented by fan state in this prototype, every 50 ms. Both adapters publish timestamped snapshots, and the controller accepts this synchronized pair. The prototype hardware pro-files specify CPU, GPU, and RAM frequencies. We construct candidate actions by pairing platform-supported hardware profiles with scheduled-token caps. 

ThermPINN implementation. We train ThermPINN on 465,300 sensor temperatures, domain power, ambient temper-ature, and cooling state, about 267 device hours. The corpus comprises four trajectories spanning four cooling conditions and 20 workload classes on three testbeds. We split the cor-pus by complete trajectory into training, validation, and test sets comprising 74.7%, 15.3%, and 10.0%, respectively. Sensor temperatures supervise the predicted field, while sampled in-terior, boundary, interface, and initial-time points enforce the PDE and associated conditions. ThermPINN can be trained with ease on an Apple MacBook in two hours. 

Online prediction and scheduling. We construct an RHP from ThermPINN’s identified thermal dynamics using POD and reduced-operator fitting. The 1 .5 MB RHP bundle con-tains reduced operators conditioned on cooling state and residuals for calibration. A dedicated worker runs every 2 s for a 64 s forecasting window to correct the RHP state and search three control intervals, using the calibrated serving-transition catalogue to predict serving behavior. Telemetry, prediction, search, or failures trigger conservative fallback, while independent safety controls remain active and failure or exit restores the startup platform state. The LLM-to-Heat Compiler, RHP, and scheduler took 18.13 ms, 24.36 ms, and 11.5 ms on average, respectively, corresponding to 0.91%, 1.22%, and 0.58% of the 2 s control interval.

6.3 Sensitivity Analysis 

We examine sensitivity to ambient temperature, prompt length, cooling state, and dataset. Each experiment changes the corresponding setting while retaining the other default settings. 

Impact of Ambient Temperature Setpoint. We evaluated ThermE with Qwen3-14B-AWQ at ambient setpoints from 30 ◦C to 73 ◦C with 100% fan speed. Fig. 15 shows that in-creasing ambient temperature raised serving latency, with larger increases above 60 ◦C. At a 512-token prompt length, TTFT increased from 4.36 s at 30 ◦C to 6.69 s at 70 ◦C, while TPOT increased from 167.36 to 290.44 ms. The other prompt lengths exhibited a similar temperature-dependent increase. Thus, ThermE maintained broadly stable serving at lower setpoints and continued processing requests as higher ambi-ent temperatures reduced capacity. Its ambient-conditioned headroom forecasts expose the shrinking thermal margin to the scheduler, allowing action selection to account for environmental heating. 

Impact of Prompt Length. We also compared 128-, 512-, and 1,024-token prompts in Fig. 15. At 30 ◦C, it shows that increasing prompt length from 128 to 1,024 tokens raised TTFT from 1.10 to 7.55 s and TPOT from 144.76 to 196.67 ms. ThermE 

Longer prompts require more prefill work and enlarge the context accessed during decoding, increasing the work in both phases. The same trend held at higher setpoints, with a wider latency gap for longer prompts. These results show that ThermE supported all three input lengths with differ-ent serving costs. Its compiler represents prompt-dependent compute and memory work, enabling the scheduler to ac-count for the corresponding heat demand when selecting hardware profiles and scheduled-token caps. 

Performance across workloads. We then test the ThermE on Chat, Code, and Summary tasks in Table 1, while other set-tings remain at their default values. Table 2 compares TTFT, TPOT, peak temperature, and GPU power with the best and worst baselines for each metric. ThermE reduced TPOT rela-tive to the strongest baseline by 28.56%, 5.46%, and 41.33% for Chat, Code, and Summary, respectively, showing a consistent decoding benefit across workloads. For Summary, ThermE reduced TTFT by up to 88.95% across baselines, although it remained 11.06% higher than the best baseline, suggesting a tradeoff in which headroom-aware scheduling sacrifices some prefill speed to sustain faster decoding. ThermE uses model and request descriptors to balance serving progress against shared-headroom consumption. 

6.4 Ablation Studies 

We evaluate five variants under the same action space and thermal guard. Without Compiler replaces the package activ-ity signature with GPU utilization and total power. Without Coupling uses independent per-zone temperature predictors. 

Without Uncertainty plans from the ensemble mean without a conformal margin. Greedy replaces horizon search with a one-step decision. Full numerical solver replaces the reduced thermal predictor with a full numerical solver. Table 3 shows that removing the compiler, thermal cou-pling, or uncertainty margin increased TTFT, TPOT, and SLO violations by 11.02–14.32%. These components jointly ac-count for workload-dependent heat generation, cross-domain propagation, and prediction uncertainty, supporting bet-ter serving decisions. Greedy increased TTFT and TPOT by 13.18% and 13.19%, respectively, and SLO violations by 15.63%, demonstrating the benefit of anticipating how cur-rent actions consume future headroom. The full numerical solver increased TTFT by 8.70%, TPOT by 26.68%, and SLO violations by 48.20%, yielding the largest decoding and SLO penalties. The reduced predictor’s compact state updates offer a plausible explanation for its serving advantage by limiting candidate-evaluation computation. Together, these results support combining predictive headroom estimation with multi-interval planning for sustained serving under thermal constraints.

## 7 Related Work 

Efficient LLM inference on heterogeneous platforms. 

vLLM improves throughput with PagedAttention [10], and POD-Attention overlaps prefill and decode attention [8]. He-lix optimizes placement and routing across heterogeneous GPUs [15]. Mobile systems exploit NPUs for prefill [29], com-bine GPU and NPU execution [16], and coordinate CPU, GPU, and memory frequencies [31]. ThermE complements these optimizations with predictive thermal control for sustained serving under limited shared headroom. 

Thermal Modeling and Headroom Prediction. 3D-ICE models nonlinear thermal transients and heat sinks [26], while DeepOHeat maps power and boundary conditions Lu et al. 

to temperature fields [14]. SAU-FNO combines attention and neural operators for thermal prediction [5]. ASRR-PINN uses adaptive sampling for physics-informed thermal anal-ysis [32]. TAPAS estimates from thermal and power histo-ries [24], while ReThermal accelerates wafer-scale thermal simulation with neural networks [12]. ThermE complements these models by translating uncertainty-calibrated forecasts into shared headroom for low-overhead serving decisions. 

Thermal-Aware Resource Management. EarDVFS adapts mobile frequency control using thermal and cooling state [9]. TAPAS coordinates placement, routing, and reconfiguration under cloud thermal and power constraints [24]. ReThermal combines thermal modeling and scheduling for liquid-cooled wafer-scale LLM training [12]. ThermE extends workload-aware thermal management to shared-cooling edge SoCs, jointly controlling hardware profiles and scheduled-token caps to preserve serving quality. 

## 8 Conclusion 

We presented ThermE, a runtime system that manages shared thermal headroom for sustained LLM inference on edge SoCs. ThermE combines LLM-to-heat compilation, physics-constrained headroom prediction, and uncertainty-aware scheduling to coordinate hardware profiles and scheduled-token caps. Evaluation demonstrated improved serving qual-ity under thermal constraints. This work contributes a predic-tive thermal resource management approach to the systems community, providing a foundation for sustained onboard intelligence in satellites, smart AI mobile devices, and other compact platforms like System-on-package (SoP) where lim-ited cooling constrains continuous inference.
