## Abstract 

Next-generation large language models (LLMs) are expand-ing from the cloud to ubiquitous edge devices. However, edge devices typically either lack the memory to store increasingly large LLM weights or, even with enough memory, spend un-affordable energy on loading the weights. This raises our question: can an edge device run an LLM without storing or loading its weights, but receive them over the air and con-sume them on the fly? Inspired by wireless broadcasting, we present AIR-LLM, an LLM inference architecture for edge de-vices, which is composed of: ( i) a central radio (e.g., 5G base stations) that broadcasts the LLM weights into the air, and (ii ) the edge user that receives the weights and completes the general matrix-vector multiplication (GEMV) of LLM inference directly in the radio frequency (RF) domain using RF mixers. To further shorten the airtime, AIR-LLM exploits MIMO spatial multiplexing and proposes an energy-efficient precoder–postcoder pair on the edge to calibrate its own wireless channel. Since the central radio stays user-unaware, AIR-LLM is user-scalable so that one broadcast serves unlim-ited users within its coverage. We implement AIR-LLM on the NVIDIA Sionna ray-traced channels of two real-world urban scenes and the profiling of a real RF mixer. With a WikiText-2 perplexity degradation of 4.0% on LLaMA-3.1-8B, AIR-LLM saves the energy by 157.7 ×/40.4 × against the FP16 and weight-only quantization baselines; with 20 users, its airtime is 104.1 ×/26.0 × shorter, respectively. 

Keywords: LLM inference, edge computing, RF computing, RF mixer, weight broadcasting, MIMO communication 

## 1 Introduction 

Trillions of edge devices today are embracing emerging machine learning (ML) techniques for a wide range of ap-plications, such as continuous health monitoring [3, 45], on-device speech recognition [49, 67], and cooperative au-tonomous driving [24, 71]. Among these, the emerging large language models (LLMs) provide a unique capability, allow-ing understanding, reasoning, and generating natural lan-guage [16, 17, 26, 59]. However, the usage of LLMs on the edge mostly relies on cloud computing, where the locally generated prompts are uploaded to a cloud server, and the re-sponses are downloaded afterwards. This again exposes two typical concerns of cloud computing: the privacy of the sen-sitive prompts, as well as the round-trip latency, especially when the number of edge users is scaled up [27, 66]. The key challenge that hinders such an on-device deploy-ment lies in the daunting model size of today’s LLMs. For example, LLaMA-3.1-8B [17] occupies 16 GB in FP16, and still 4 GB after 4-bit weight-only quantization (WoQ) [13, 33]. Such a model size stresses an edge device in two stages. First, its memory is typically too small to hold the whole model, so a local deployment has to keep downloading the weights and discarding them afterwards. Second, even if an edge device could store such an LLM entirely, the energy of loading the weights from memory to the computing unit is unaffordable. This is because LLM inference is memory-bounded, espe-cially during the decoding phase, where fetching one weight feeds only two floating-point operations (FLOPs) [18]. The weight movement therefore dominates the energy, much more than the arithmetic it feeds [6, 43]. To overcome this, various computing paradigms have been proposed recently. For example, in-memory computing com-pletes the computing in analog within the memory itself, on a ReRAM [8, 52, 61], PCM [28, 31], or SRAM [34] crossbar, so that the weights are never fetched. Over-the-air comput-ing [41, 75], in turn, superposes the signals of many trans-mitters over the wireless channel, so that the summation is completed by the channel itself. However, in both cases the size of the general matrix-vector multiplication (GEMV) is frozen and limited by the hardware complexity, e.g., a crossbar of 128 ×128 cells [52] and an array of a few dozen antennas [50]. Such a limited GEMV size is naturally incom-patible with the LLM whose GEMV sizes keep increasing. Our motivation lies in the wireless network these edge users already live in. Taking 5G networks as an example, a 5G base station already establishes wireless links to each edge user under its coverage. For these users, accessing the LLM weights is read-only, and thus a purely downlink pro-cess; once they run the same LLM, it is exactly what broad-casting was invented for: one transmission simultaneously serving every user in the coverage. Moreover, we employ radio-frequency (RF) computing [14, 15], which completes the computing in RF via the passive RF mixer that every re-ceiver already carries. The computing is thereby finished in analog once the broadcast signal arrives, without the weights ever being digitized into the local memory. arXiv:2610.00465v1 [cs.IT] 30 Sep 2026 Gao et al.

System overview of AIR-LLM: a central radio frequency-encodes the LLM weights onto an OFDM wave-form and broadcasts it over the air to serve any edge user within the coverage; the edge user drives the LO with its private activations, and the RF mixer’s output directly carries the GEMV outputs. The LLM inference is completed once the weight waveform arrives, without ever storing it in memory. As shown in Fig. 1, we present AIR-LLM, an LLM infer-ence architecture with ( i) a central radio (e.g., a 5G base station) that frequency-encodes the LLM weights onto the subcarriers of an orthogonal frequency-division multiplex-ing (OFDM) waveform and broadcasts it, and ( ii ) any edge device in the coverage receives that waveform and computes on it inside its own RF front-end. The key computing compo-nent is the RF mixer every wireless device already carries: it multiplies its two inputs in the time domain, corresponding to a linear convolution in the frequency domain. Specifically, driving its local oscillator (LO) with a waveform encoding the activations therefore convolves the arriving weight spectrum with the activation spectrum, and the very middle subcarrier of this convolution is exactly the inner product of the weight and activation vectors. A comparison of existing computing paradigms is presented in Tab. 1. On top of this principle, AIR-LLM makes two designs. First, it exploits 𝐴 × 𝐴 multiple-input multiple-output (MIMO) spa-tial multiplexing, which delivers 𝐴 weight streams at a time and thus shortens the airtime by 𝐴 ×. Second, it calibrates the MIMO channel with 𝐶 calibration units, each pairing a per-subcarrier precoder and a per-antenna postcoder, which compensates for the channel at a cost far below an explicit inversion, keeping the energy low. Delivering the weights this way also fits broadcasting bet-ter than conventional communication in two aspects. First, AIR-LLM needs no handshake with users, as the computa-tion is inherently tolerant to the errors introduced by thermal noise. In addition, its weight waveform stays identical re-gardless of the users’ SNRs, so no per-user modulation and coding scheme (MCS) is needed either. We implement AIR-LLM on ray-traced channels of two ur-ban scenes, Paris and Munich, and profile a real RF mixer, run-ning nine LLMs from 1B to 70B parameters end-to-end with every weight-activation GEMV in RF computing. On LLaMA-3.1-8B and a selected user in the scene of Paris, AIR-LLM degrades the WikiText-2 perplexity (PPL) by only 4.0%, while cutting the per-token energy by 157.7 ×/40.4 × against FP16/4-bit weight baselines with remote weight storage, and by 51.5 ×/13.9 × against fetching them from local memory; with 20 users, its per-token airtime is 104.1 ×/26.0 × shorter than FP16 and 4-bit WoQ. We summarize the contribution of AIR-LLM as below. 

• We propose AIR-LLM, a new computing paradigm for LLM inference on the edge, which is memory-access-free ,

user-scalable , and energy-efficient .

• We design an RF computing engine that shortens the air-time with MIMO spatial multiplexing, while maintaining the computing accuracy and energy efficiency. 

• We evaluate AIR-LLM on Sionna ray-tracing and real RF mixer profiling, showing its near-lossless PPL on LLM tasks with orders-of-magnitude less energy and airtime.

3.4 System Efficiency Analysis 

We analyze the efficiency of AIR-LLM along two dimensions: the airtime it occupies the wireless channel, and the energy it consumes on the edge device. 

Airtime analysis. The airtime of AIR-LLM is inversely proportional to the number of antennas 𝐴 and the occu-pied bandwidth 𝐵 . Interestingly, this form exactly follows a conventional communication link with spatial multiplex-ing on 𝐴 and frequency multiplexing on 𝐵 . On the other hand, AIR-LLM’s airtime is independent of the SNR, which instead determines the computing accuracy; AIR-LLM there-fore does not customize the transmitted signals for each user, and is naturally fit to broadcasting. Leveraged by broadcast-ing, one transmission of the weights serves arbitrarily many edge users at a constant airtime, so the airtime amortized per user decreases as 1 /𝑈 . The complete analysis is given in Appendix C.3. 

Energy analysis. Overall, AIR-LLM achieves good energy efficiency on RF computing, whose energy per FLOP is fur-ther averaged down as the GEMV scales up to LLM. This is because the energy consumption of almost every hardware component in AIR-LLM is amortized by either the input size 

𝑁 or the output size 𝑀 , leaving the DAC, whose per-FLOP energy is set by the antenna number 𝐴 and the unit num-ber 𝐶 , as the floor that a large GEMV converges to. Such a scaling law is contributed by two key designs. First, the RF mixer and the LPF collapse the whole input dimension into a single subcarrier with a downsampling ratio of 𝑁 , so the ADC takes only one sample per inner product, which usu-ally dominates the energy budget of an analog computing system; the postcoding runs on that same single sample per antenna, so its energy is amortized by 𝑁 as well. Second, the precoding and the IFFT are paid only once per GEMV, as the same precoded input signal x′ is reused across all the 

⌈𝑀 /( 2𝐴 )⌉ rounds, so their energy is amortized by the output size 𝑀 . The per-component analysis is in Appendix C.4. 

Tradeoff on computing accuracy, airtime, and energy. The calibration unit number 𝐶 is where these three metrics meet. On the accuracy side, OPT-Chan calibrates the channel with a rank-𝐶 approximation, so a larger 𝐶 leaves a smaller resid-ual and thus a more accurate channel calibration; and the more antennas 𝐴 are deployed, the larger 𝐶 this accuracy requires. On the energy side, however, every energy term grows linearly with 𝐶 . Scaling up 𝐴 therefore shortens the airtime but pays for its accuracy in energy, so 𝐶 should be the smallest that meets the target task’s accuracy.

4.2 Hardware Profiling on Real RF Mixer 

RF mixers are not built for analog multiplication, and hence distort the desired product. We therefore measure the dis-tortion of a real RF mixer, ZEM-4300+ [37], driven by USRP X310 radios [12] as the transceivers, as shown in Fig. 4(c). The profiling is wired, so the power entering the mixer stays far above the noise floor; the link is thus considered noiseless, and the computing errors are fully attributed to the mixer’s own distortion. Specifically, for each power at the mixer’s RF port, which receives the weight signal over the air in a deployment, we sweep the power at its LO port and keep the lowest distortion error, i.e., the best effective number of bits (ENOB), that the mixer reaches. The measured distor-tion ENOB curve over the RF power is shown in Fig. A2 of Appendix E.1. In our evaluation, we read this curve at each edge device’s received weight signal power and plug in the corresponding distortion error.

## 6 Related Works 

LLM accelerators. Serving an LLM is memory-bounded by the weight movement rather than arithmetic [18]. One cat-egory of works shrinks the bits per weight and co-designs the compute units around the resulting mixed-format GEMVs [13, 33, 60, 65], with the hardware absorbing the outliers [19, 32] or the dequantization [39, 46, 64]; a second attacks the move-ment rather than the format, placing the compute next to the memory that holds the weights and the KV cache [21, 47, 69]. All share one premise: the weights are already stored in the device’s memory, and only the cost of fetching and multi-plying a resident weight is optimized. AIR-LLM removes that premise: no weight is stored on the edge device; each arrival of the broadcast weight waveform is consumed and computed directly in the RF/analog domain. 

Analog computing for ML inference. Analog comput-ing performs the FLOPs in physics rather than in digital, escaping the energy floor of digital arithmetic: in-memory computing completes a GEMV on a ReRAM, PCM, or SRAM crossbar through Ohm’s and Kirchhoff’s laws [31, 52, 61], while photonic computing multiplies operands encoded onto light [53, 73, 76]. However, such designs are usually limited by their analog error and readout converters [28, 72], yet already carry real models at a handful of effective bits [5, 63, 74]; AIR-LLM likewise reports every result at the effective bits its link resolves [11]. In addition, AIR-LLM generalizes the single-antenna and single-weight-stream into an 𝐴 × 𝐴 MIMO engine to largely reduce the airtime, whose calibration units zero-force a real, frequency-selective channel. 

LLM deployment on the edge. On-device LLM serving is a capacity problem before a compute one, and the sys-tems response keeps as few weights resident as possible: pruning [42], activation sparsity [57, 62], flash streaming [4], or accelerator–model co-design [68]. Yet even a quantized resident model stays memory-bound on commodity phones, where energy and thermals make continuous generation un-tenable [30]. The complementary response moves the model off the device [27, 29, 58], trading the memory wall for a per-query network cost and for handing the prompt to the server. Both leave each device paying, privately and per token, for the same weights that every other device in the cell also pays for [48]. AIR-LLM instead disaggregates the weight storage to the central radio while keeping both the computation and the prompt on the edge, and because its transmission is edge-device-unaware, one broadcast is mixed by every device in the coverage at once, amortizing the delivery cost over the devices served. Such one-to-many analog delivery has precedent in SoftCast [25], which broadcasts video over raw OFDM at a fidelity set by each receiver’s own channel. 

## 7 Conclusion 

In this paper, we present AIR-LLM, an LLM inference ar-chitecture for edge users that is memory-access-free , user-scalable and energy-efficient . Specifically, AIR-LLM exploits MIMO spatial multiplexing to deliver multiple weight streams at a time and thereby shorten the airtime, and the design of the precoder–postcoder pair on the edge user energy-efficiently calibrates its own wireless channel. Exhaustive evaluations on the NVIDIA Sionna ray-traced channels and the profiling of a real RF mixer reveal that, with a slight PPL gap to the FP16 baseline, AIR-LLM cuts the per-token energy and airtime by orders of magnitude. We believe AIR-LLM opens up a new path for the next generation of edge intelli-gence. Gao et al.

between the two scenes is the price of the per-block channel approximation of (19): the richer multipath of Munich makes the frequency response vary within a block, so the averaged ¯𝐻 that Step 1 inverts sits further from the per-subcarrier channel that (17) actually scores, and the SVD initialization alone is worth about one bit there against three in Paris.

## C Airtime and Energy Consumption 

This section analyzes the two efficiency metrics of AIR-LLM, the airtime and the energy consumption per FLOP. We derive them at the general block size of a BCO (§C.1 and §C.2), and then substitute the GEMV mapping of the main body, its degenerate case, to read off the airtime and the energy of a GEMV (§C.3 and §C.4). 

C.1 Airtime of a BCO

## D AIR-LLM in CNN 

Convolutional (CONV) layers, which dominate CNNs, do not fit the one-subcarrier-per-output mapping of the main body: reusing the same kernel across all 𝐼 2 positions of an image would require re-transmitting that kernel once per output pixel, so the broadcast weight traffic, the very re-source AIR-LLM is built to save, would grow by 𝐼 2. The BCO of Appendix B removes that restriction, as one mixing round now retains a whole output block instead of a single subcar-rier, and a kernel broadcast once is correlated against every position of the image within it. We map a Conv2D layer onto the BCO (§D.1), and then specialize the BCO airtime of §C.1 and the BCO energy of §C.2 to it (§D.2 and §D.3).

This section details the baselines, the tasks, and the metrics of the evaluation, together with the two measurements that the implementation of §4 rests on: the distortion profile of the RF mixer (Fig. A2) and the path-loss distribution of each ray-traced scene (Fig. A3). 

E.1 Measured Distortion and Path Loss Distortion profile of the RF mixer. Fig. A2 reports the wired sweep of §4: at each power delivered to the mixer’s RF port, the LO power is swept and the best ENOB is kept. The ENOB climbs with the RF power and then flattens as the mixer’s own nonlinearity takes over from the drive level; this knee is the distortion floor that the saturated users of §5.3 sit on. 

Path loss across the candidate devices. Fig. A3 shows the PL distribution of the 100 candidate devices dropped in each scene, from which the representative devices of §5.1 are selected. Both scenes therefore span the range of link qualities from the noise-limited users of §5.3 to the distortion-limited ones. 

E.2 Baselines 

We benchmark AIR-LLM against digital baselines along two dimensions: the precision at which the weights are kept, and the weight storage from which they reach the edge device. 

Precision dimension. We take FP16 as the exact refer-ence, and further consider weight-only quantization (WoQ) at 4/3 bit, i.e., W4A16 and W3A16, by RTN, GPTQ [13], and AWQ [33]. 

Analog computing baselines. We further benchmark against two analog computing baselines at the same ENOB as AIR-LLM’s device: ( i) AWGN , additive Gaussian noise on each GEMV output, and ( ii ) HyFlexPIM , a multiplicative Gaussian error on each weight, i.e., the conductance spread of a memristive crossbar. The former error is redrawn per GEMV, whereas the latter is frozen on the weights; AIR-LLM sits in between, as its thermal noise is redrawn per transmis-sion while its calibration residual and mixer distortion hold for as long as the channel does. 

Weight storage dimension. Under remote storage, the weights are kept on the central radio and delivered to the edge device over the same wireless channel as AIR-LLM’s by a standard communication link. Under local storage, they are instead pre-stored in the edge device’s memory. AIR-LLM targets the remote storage, which is both more realistic for edge computing and more challenging than the local one.

Zero-shot accuracy using LLaMA-3.1-8B. We evaluate AIR-LLM and the same set of baselines on LLaMA-3.1-8B’s inference on six commonsense-reasoning benchmarks, as shown in Tab. A8. This metric is harsher on analog com-puting than the PPL, because of how it is scored: every can-didate continuation is concatenated to the same stem and run through its own inference pass, and the answer is the candidate with the highest log-likelihood. An error that is deterministic given the weights, the quantization grid of WoQ or the frozen conductance spread of HyFlexPIM, shifts all the candidates of a question in the same direction, so the comparison among them, and thus the argmax, largely sur-vives. A non-deterministic one does not: the AWGN baseline and AIR-LLM’s thermal noise are redrawn independently in every pass, so each candidate is scored under its own realization of the error, and the choice flips whenever that perturbation exceeds the log-likelihood margin between the candidates. AWGN pays for this in full, collapsing to the chance level on all six tasks (37.98% at ENOB 4 and 36.30% at ENOB 3) despite an ENOB matched to AIR-LLM’s, whereas the weight-frozen HyFlexPIM stays within 0.42 points of the FP16 reference at ENOB 4. AIR-LLM sits between the two, averaging 68.72% in Paris and 65.67% in Munich against the 75.00% FP16 reference, as only its thermal noise is redrawn per transmission while its channel-calibration residual and the mixer’s distortion hold across the candidates of a ques-tion: Paris sits inside the W3A16 band (66.00–69.96%) and Munich 0.33 points below its weakest member. Task by task, the degradation is mild where the margins between the can-didate continuations are wide (77.85%/75.39% on HellaSwag against 78.89%, and 81.13%/75.20% on BoolQ against 81.96%), and the average is dominated by WinoGrande (57.77%/57.14% against 73.56%), whose two candidates differ in a single pro-noun referent and whose log-likelihood margin is therefore the narrowest of the six, so a few bits of analog error are enough to flip the choice.

Impact of the carrier frequency. We repeat the trans-mit power sweep of Tab. 4 in two other bands, the 915 MHz ISM band over 25 MHz and the 28 GHz mmWave band over 400 MHz, on the same two edge devices at the same posi-tions and under the same LLaMA-3.1-8B setup, as shown in Tab. A10. The channels are re-traced at each carrier with the same scenes, whose ITU-R P.2040 material models are extrapolated below their 1 GHz lower edge for 915 MHz, and the noise floor follows the bandwidth of each band. Since the path loss of the two devices drops by 12 dB at 915 MHz and grows by 32 dB (Paris) and 41 dB (Munich) at 28 GHz, the power axis is shifted accordingly, with the regulatory ceilings of 36 dBm EIRP in the ISM band and 75 dBm per 100 MHz at 28 GHz inside the swept range. In the ISM band, both devices reach the accuracy of the paper’s operating point at 20 dB less power: Munich reads 7.28 at 30 dBm against the 7.37 that Tab. 4 measures at 50 dBm, while Paris has already saturated at 6.72. Both therefore stay usable inside the regulatory ceil-ing, reading 6.70 and 6.74 at 35 dBm, and the band’s own floors (6.69 in Paris and 6.44 in Munich) even reverse the two devices’ order, the lower carrier reaching the more distant device better than the nearer one. What the band costs is airtime: its 25 MHz is a quarter of the bandwidth 𝐵 that (26) divides, so every GEMV occupies four times the air. At 28 GHz the trade reverses. Paris needs 80 dBm to sat-urate, 30 dB more than at 3.5 GHz, and saturates 9% worse, at a floor of 7.05 against 6.48; this gap is a distortion floor that no transmit power removes, the sparser multipath of the band leaving each per-subcarrier channel matrix worse con-ditioned for the calibration units to invert. Munich, another 17 dB of path loss away (a gap the band itself widens, from 7.9 dB at 3.5 GHz to 16.7 dB here), never becomes usable at all: 170.87 at the regulatory ceiling and still 9.98 at 100 dBm, i.e., 19 dB beyond what the band is allowed to radiate. Its 400 MHz does cut the airtime fourfold, but only for a device close enough to the central radio to spend it. Across the three bands, therefore, the carrier frequency trades airtime against coverage almost one for one, and AIR-LLM is the more comfortable the lower the band, the opposite of where the spectrum for it is easiest to find.
