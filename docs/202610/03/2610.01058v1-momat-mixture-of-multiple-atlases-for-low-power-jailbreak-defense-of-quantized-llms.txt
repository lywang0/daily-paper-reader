A B S T R A C T 

Quantized large language models are increasingly deployed on edge devices for their low latency and energy efficiency. However, model quantization weakens alignment safeguards, leaving qLLMs (quantized large language models) highly vulnerable to jailbreak attacks. To address this challenge, we present MOMAT (Mixture of Multiple Atlases), a hardware-enhanced safety framework that combines structured knowledge retrieval with low-power defense acceleration. Each atlas represents a semantic cluster of harmful or benign sample sets and policy templates, enabling domain-localized Retrieval-Augmented Generation guarding that mitigates the curse of dimensionality and the resulting semantic sparsity problem in large, heterogeneous safety databases. MOMAT retrieves top-𝑘 similarity features from all atlases for each prompt and evaluates them using a lightweight MoE (Mixture of Experts) detector, while a CiM (Compute-in-Memory)-accelerated similarity engine performs fast, low-power atlas-local retrieval. MOMAT’s CiM-based retrieval accelerates a 100-query batch from 15,052.44 ms to 3,207.21 ns (a 4.69 × 10 6× speedup) and reduces energy from 8.1 × 10 7𝜇 J to 3.32 𝜇 J, yielding a 2.5 × 10 5× energy reduction over DRAM-based (Raspberry Pi) baselines. Red-team evaluations across standard benchmarks show that MOMAT matches the defense performance of state-of-the-art methods while avoiding benign overkill and providing substantial efficiency gains, demonstrating that CiM-based modular defenses can make edge-deployed qLLMs both safer and more energy-efficient. We will release the full 223.2k-sample dataset to foster future research. 

## 1. Introduction 

Quantized large language models (qLLMs) have become increasingly attractive for edge and embedded deployment because quantization significantly reduces model size, la-tency, and energy consumption (Lin et al., 2024; Xiao et al., 2023a). However, these advantages degrade alignment robustness (Kharinaev et al., 2025). Compressing model weights distorts the representation geometry and shrinks safety-relevant feature subspaces, while activation clipping during quantization further compounds these effects (Xu et al., 2024). Together, these changes reduce the model’s resistance to jailbreaks, prompt injection, and role-playing attacks (Ren et al., 2024). Edge-deployed qLLMs are thus far more vulnerable than their full-precision cloud counterparts (Egashira et al., 2024), yet most run without dedicated safety mechanisms (Yi et al., 2025). Efficient defense frameworks 

In this work, we show that restructuring safety retrieval into domain-localized atlases and accelerating similarity search with compute-in-memory hardware can recover alignment robustness lost to quantization while meeting the latency and energy constraints of edge deployment. Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) can help mit-igate jailbreaks by retrieving refusal templates or policy constraints (Wang et al., 2025). But this approach struggles in large, heterogeneous safety databases. As the diversity of harmful intents grows, the embedding space becomes sparse. The curse of dimensionality means that semantically unrelated items end up at similar distances from the query. A safety-critical query may fail to retrieve the right exemplars and instead surface irrelevant or weakly related templates. This retrieval noise not only weakens downstream safety responses, but adversaries can also exploit it to bypass tem-plate matching entirely. Edge devices face an additional chal-lenge: they lack the memory bandwidth for high-capacity vector search, which increases both latency and energy costs. A more structured approach is needed—one that uses domain-localized retrieval rather than a single monolithic database. We introduce MOMAT (Mixture of Multiple Atlases), a hardware-accelerated safety framework that restructures the retrieval problem into multiple semantically coherent sub-spaces. Rather than using a single large safety database, MO-MAT organizes harmful and benign samples and policy tem-plates into specialized atlases. This reduces cross-domain interference and improves nearest-neighbor reliability by operating within compact clusters. For each input prompt, the CiM engine retrieves top-𝑘 simi-larity features from all atlases in parallel, enabling domain-localized retrieval with much less search noise. For edge deployment, MOMAT uses a CiM (Compute-in-Memory)-enhanced RAG engine that performs similarity search di-rectly in memory arrays. This lowers both latency and energy consumption compared to DRAM-based vector retrieval. Together, MOMAT provides a modular safety architecture for defending qLLMs under edge constraints. ## 2. Background 

2.1. Quantization and Alignment Collapse 

Quantization is a widely used technique for accelerating LLM inference on memory- and energy-constrained edge 

B. Li et al.: Preprint submitted to Elsevier Page 2 of 11 MOMAT: Mixture of Multiple Atlases for Low-Power Jailbreak Defense of Quantized LLMs 

hardware (Lin et al., 2024). By reducing numerical precision through weight compression and activation quantization, qLLMs achieve substantial reductions in model size, in-ference latency, and power consumption. However, these efficiency gains come at the cost of reduced representa-tional fidelity (Qi et al., 2025). Quantization disrupts high-dimensional feature geometry by introducing weight round-ing errors and activation outlier clipping, which compress semantic distances and flatten safety-critical gradients in deeper transformer layers (Zhang et al., 2025). As a result, the low-rank subspaces that encode safety-relevant informa-tion are degraded, making it harder for the model to separate harmful from benign intent in embedding space (Wei et al., 2024). The safety layers responsible for recognizing and rejecting adversarial inputs are disproportionately affected, as they rely on fine-grained representational distinctions that aggressive quantization erodes (Zhao et al., 2024). Quantized models are consequently far more susceptible to jailbreaks (Li et al., 2025), prompt injection, and multi-turn steering compared to their full-precision counterparts (Hong et al., 2024). This vulnerability is compounded in edge deployments, where qLLMs typically run in isolation without auxiliary safety models, server-side monitoring, or high-bandwidth retrieval modules. Alignment collapse un-der quantization is therefore a critical and underaddressed vulnerability in real-world edge scenarios. 

2.2. Jailbreak and Prompt Injection Threat 

Jailbreak and prompt-injection attacks coerce LLMs into generating harmful or policy-violating content by exploit-ing the model’s reliance on natural-language cues rather than formal safety guarantees (Deng et al., 2024; Walker et al., 2025). These attacks span a broad taxonomy: role-playing and persona hijacking (Li et al., 2024), multi-turn context steering, translation-based obfuscation (Zhou et al., 2024), and adversarially paraphrased instructions (Guo et al., 2024). Prompt-injection variants can override system messages, invert safety rules, or hijack in-context exemplars, effectively reprogramming the model’s behavior at inference time without modifying its weights. Quantized LLMs are especially vulnerable to these at-tacks. Weight and activation compression reduces robust-ness to linguistic variation, causing semantically adversarial inputs to be misclassified as benign in the distorted em-bedding space. Crucially, most jailbreak attacks require no privileged access and can be launched through standard user-facing interfaces, making them a practical and low-barrier threat for edge-deployed qLLMs that lack server-side filtering or safety oversight. 

2.3. Limitations of Safety RAG: Curse of Dimensionality and Semantic Sparsity 

RAG is commonly used to improve LLM safety by retrieving refusal templates, safety exemplars, or policy snippets at inference time (Walker et al., 2025). However, this approach degrades as the safety database grows large and semantically heterogeneous. As harmful-intent prompts diversify across categories, the embedding space grows in-creasingly sparse. The curse of dimensionality causes se-mantically unrelated vectors to converge to similar distances from a query point, undermining nearest-neighbor relia-bility (Kouiroukidis and Evangelidis, 2011). Safety-critical queries consequently fail to retrieve the correct refusal ex-emplars and instead surface irrelevant, weakly related, or overly generic templates that are ineffective against nuanced jailbreak attempts. The problem is further compounded when exemplars from semantically distant domains—such as self-harm, il-licit instructions, harassment, and privacy leakage—coexist in a shared embedding index. This mixture introduces cross-domain interference that amplifies retrieval noise and de-grades the precision of template-based safety mechanisms. Beyond retrieval quality, large monolithic RAG indices impose significant memory bandwidth demands: DRAM-based vector search requires repeated high-latency data transfers that are incompatible with the real-time safety enforcement requirements of edge hardware. Monolithic RAG designs are therefore poorly suited for qLLM safety on resource-constrained platforms, motivating a more struc-tured, domain-localized retrieval approach. 

2.4. Edge Deployment Constraints and CiM-Based Accelerators 

Edge and embedded platforms impose stringent con-straints on memory capacity, bandwidth, and energy con-sumption (Friha et al., 2024), making it difficult to deploy safety mechanisms that depend on large retrieval indices or compute-heavy classifiers. DRAM-based vector search is particularly costly: each similarity query requires multi-ple high-latency memory accesses (Chen et al., 2025; Ko et al., 2025), and the energy spent moving data between memory and compute units dominates the overall retrieval cost (Horowitz, 2014). Under the traditional von Neumann architecture, this memory wall prevents edge devices from sustaining the retrieval throughput that real-time safety en-forcement demands (Sharma et al., 2025). Compute-in-Memory (CiM) architectures offer a prin-cipled solution by performing Multiply-Accumulate (MAC) operations directly within memory arrays, eliminating the dominant source of data movement overhead (Ali et al., 2022; Yin et al., 2024; Qin et al., 2024). CiM is particularly well-suited for retrieval workloads because nearest-neighbor search reduces to vector-matrix multiplication: query em-beddings are broadcast as input vectors, the stored exemplar matrix resides in the crossbar array, and similarity scores are produced in a single analog MAC sweep without trans-ferring data off-chip. This architectural alignment between retrieval computation and CiM hardware enables orders-of-magnitude reductions in both latency and energy per query compared to DRAM-based designs (Sharma et al., 2025; Yu et al., 2021). MOMAT exploits this property by storing atlas-specific exemplar matrices directly in dedicated CiM arrays, en-abling fast, low-power similarity search over compact,

## 3. Method 

3.1. System Overview 

MOMAT is a CiM–assisted safety framework that re-stores alignment behaviors sacrificed in quantized LLMs deployed on edge hardware. MOMAT decouples prompt en-coding from the protected qLLM: input prompts are encoded by a lightweight, encoder-only embedding model ( ; we use BAAI/bge-large-en-v1.5 (Xiao et al., 2023b), 0.3B param-eters), which is frozen throughout and serves solely to pro-duce stable, full-precision embeddings for safety processing. This component can be replaced by any embedding model and is independent of the qLLM being defended. Rather than querying a domain-agnostic retrieval corpus, MOMAT organizes safety knowledge into multiple semantic atlases, enabling localized retrieval and reducing interference from unrelated domains. As shown in Fig. 1, the system comprises four modules: a CiM retrieval module that computes top-𝑘 

similarity features from each atlas for every incoming query, atlas-specific safety stores that maintain dense CiM arrays of harmful exemplars and benign counterparts organized into semantically coherent clusters, a lightweight MoE (Mixture of Experts) intent detector that fuses atlas-retrieved features with the prompt embedding to produce a safety decision, and a CiM-accelerated RAG engine that serves two roles: com-puting atlas-local top-𝑘 similarity features for every query to support MoE safety scoring, and retrieving safe response templates directly within memory arrays when a harmful prompt is detected—both performed with low latency and energy suitable for edge hardware. Given a prompt, MOMAT encodes it using the frozen embedding model  (Xiao et al., 2023b) and feeds the resulting 1024-dimensional embedding to the CiM engine, which retrieves top-𝑘 exemplars from each of the 𝑀 atlases. Each atlas maintains dense clusters of harmful exemplars, policy snippets, and ICL (In-Context Learning)-generated variants organized within a dedicated CiM array. This struc-tured atlas-local retrieval confines downstream processing to semantically relevant regions and suppresses cross-domain retrieval noise. A compact MoE detector then receives the prompt embedding concatenated with atlas-local top-𝑘 pre-computed features, and produces a binary safety decision. When a harmful intent is detected, MOMAT enforces a safety response; otherwise, the prompt is passed through to the underlying qLLM. Throughout inference, the CiM engine operates in two modes: it computes atlas-local top-

𝑘 similarity features 𝑟 𝑖 (𝑥 ) for every query to support MoE scoring, and retrieves the safe response template from the most activated atlas when harmful intent is confirmed, both entirely within CiM arrays and without DRAM-based data movement, enabling sub-millisecond, low-energy retrieval suitable for edge deployment. This combination of domain-localized retrieval and CiM-based acceleration yields a modular defense pipeline that preserves the computational compactness of qLLMs while reinstating reliable safety enforcement. MOMAT amortizes the cost of safety reasoning across two phases: an offline atlas construction phase that handles all expen-sive operations including safety corpus processing, semantic clustering, and multi-stage ICL expansion, and a lightweight online inference phase that reduces to a single embedding computation followed by a compact MoE forward pass. At inference time, the CiM arrays produce atlas-local top-𝑘 

similarity signals in-situ, eliminating the need for DRAM-based vector search. This offline–online separation is key to meeting the latency and energy budgets of edge platforms, where neither large safety models nor high-bandwidth mem-ory are available. 

3.2. Atlas Construction 

MOMAT constructs multiple atlases to replace the flat, domain-agnostic vector store used in conventional RAG systems. The enrichment yields dense neighborhoods well suited for stable similarity search on CiM systems. Algorithm 1 builds atlases in three steps: (1) use a small set of seed prompts to ICL-generate cross-domain harmful–benign pairs; (2) recursively fan out each pair with paraphrasing, syntactic variation, semantic abstraction, and detailed expansions to obtain dense variant sets; (3) as-semble these variants into atlas-specific corpora, yielding domain-labeled, high-density neighborhoods. These high-density neighborhoods reduce retrieval noise and align naturally with CiM-executed similarity search. Partitioning the corpus into multiple atlases also avoids high-dimensional interference that occurs when semantically un-related domains share a single retrieval index, allowing atlas-local RAG to outperform monolithic retrieval in both accuracy and efficiency. Atlas embeddings and statistics are precomputed offline, leaving only prompt encoding, CiM atlas retrieval, and MoE scoring for the online stage.

## 4. Experiment 

This section evaluates MOMAT under realistic edge deployment conditions and quantized LLM settings. Our goal is to characterize three aspects of the system: (1)

Safety robustness, i.e., whether MOMAT recovers the align-ment capabilities that quantization erodes; (2) Deployment efficiency, i.e., whether CiM-based in-memory retrieval achieves the latency and energy targets required for edge inference; and (3) Utility preservation, whether MOMAT maintains benign performance without over-blocking. To cleanly separate algorithmic improvements from hardware acceleration, we conduct experiments across two hardware environments and multiple qLLM configurations, using multilingual jailbreak and prompt-injection benchmarks that correspond directly to MOMAT’s atlas taxonomy. 

4.1. Evaluation Settings 

We evaluate MOMAT across hardware, model, and task conditions that reflect realistic edge deployment. To separate algorithmic gains from hardware acceleration, we use two environments. On the Raspberry Pi 5, we run the full MO-MAT pipeline (CiM atlas retrieval and MoE intent detec-tion) while keeping retrieval DRAM-bound. This configu-ration isolates MOMAT’s structural advantages, specifically atlas-localized retrieval versus monolithic RAG, under the memory bandwidth and latency constraints typical of CPU-only embedded platforms. On the CiM evaluation board, we preserve the identical MOMAT control flow but exe-cute retrieval through a 4-bit/8-bit in-memory MAC array, isolating the benefit of CiM-accelerated similarity search without introducing confounding algorithmic changes. To-gether, these two platforms probe MOMAT’s two primary design axes: semantic retrieval reliability (Raspberry Pi) and energy-efficient in-memory retrieval (CiM). We experiment with two representative qLLMs—Llama-2-7B and Mistral-7B—covering common quantization regimes used for edge inference. These models display different degrees of representation distortion and alignment collapse under quantization, allowing us to assess how effectively MOMAT compensates for safety degradation across varying compression levels. To assess safety performance, we conduct red-team evaluations using domain-specific harmful instruction sets spanning categories such as weapons, self-harm, and privacy violations. These categories are aligned with the atlas taxon-omy introduced in Sec. 3.2, enabling fine-grained analysis of MOMAT’s domain-localized retrieval and its ability to surface contextually appropriate safety exemplars for each threat category. FRR measures the fraction of benign queries incorrectly refused by the safety system, capturing the over-refusal problem that plagues many conservative defenses; a lower FRR indicates that the system preserves normal qLLM utility for legitimate users. ## 5. Conclusion 

Quantized LLMs enable efficient edge deployment but suffer significant alignment degradation, making them highly susceptible to jailbreak and prompt-injection attacks. Our experiments confirm that this vulnerability is scale-dependent: while 13B models absorb W4A8 quantization with negligible ASR change, 7B models—the only scale practically deployable on edge hardware—exhibit consistent alignment collapse across datasets. This creates a funda-mental tension between the compression required for edge deployment and the representational capacity required for reliable safety alignment. Flat, domain-agnostic retrieval databases exacerbate this problem. Under the curse of di-mensionality, sparse high-dimensional embedding spaces prevent monolithic RAG systems from reliably surfacing relevant safety exemplars, particularly when the underlying qLLM’s embedding geometry has already been distorted by quantization. MOMAT addresses both failure modes by restructuring safety retrieval into semantically coherent, domain-localized atlases and executing similarity search on a CiM-accelerated engine. By confining retrieval to compact subspaces, MO-MAT preserves stable local neighborhoods even under quantization-induced embedding distortion. By offloading similarity search to CiM arrays, MOMAT achieves a 4.69 × 10 6× latency reduction and a ∼ 2 .5 × 10 5× energy reduction over DRAM-based baselines. Critically, MOMAT reduces ASR to 0.0% across both Llama2-7B and Mistral-7B under W4A8 quantization without introducing any additional over-refusal ( Δ FRR = +0 .0% ), demonstrating that strong safety enforcement and benign utility preservation are not mutually exclusive under edge constraints. These results suggest that reliable safety for edge-deployed qLLMs requires co-designing the retrieval struc-ture and the underlying hardware substrate—a principle that generalizes beyond MOMAT to the broader challenge of deploying trustworthy AI on resource-constrained devices. Future work will investigate adaptive atlas construction to handle evolving threat taxonomies, and extend MOMAT’s evaluation to a wider range of quantization schemes and on-device deployment environments.
