## Evaluating Inference Compute for Generative AI: A Framework for Enterprise Workloads 

Abbas Raza Ali * 1 Muhammad Ajmal Siddiqui 2 Moona Zahid 3

Abstract 

LLM deployment is shifting from single-turn completion to agentic trajectories in which a model plans, calls tools, reads results and reasons at test time before acting. This inverts the economics of inference hardware: chat serving amortises weight reads across large batches, whereas agent trajectories are sequentially dependent, run at effective batch one, and make per-token decode latency (TPOT) the dominant term in task completion time. Using a roofline analysis and a closed-form episode-latency model, we show why this regime favours accelerators that keep weights in on-die SRAM (Cerebras WSE-3/3T, Groq/NVIDIA LPU) or compiler-managed tiered memory (SambaNova SN40L/SN50), and why three vendor ecosystems converged in 2026 on disaggregated prefill/decode serving. We show that per-step reliability compounds exponentially in trajectory length-a 2% per-step failure rate erases a 2× decode advantage for a 20-step agent-so determinism and tail latency are first-order performance variables. We then propose a four-layer evaluation framework (silicon, serving system, agent episode, enterprise) with a metric set built on goodput at an agentic SLO and cost per successful episode, a six-axis benchmark protocol over six task families, a paired-bootstrap statistical design, an attestation protocol for vendor-run benchmarks, and TCO, availability and adoption-timing models with explicit break-even conditions. All performance figures are public and labelled by evidence class; we state seven falsifiable hypotheses and the experiments that test them, and argue that the most likely original result is that token-throughput rankings diverge from cost-per-successful-task rankings on long-horizon work. 

1. Introduction 

Inference has become the dominant cost of deployed AI [D] : Cerebras’s 2026 registration statement discloses a 750 MW inference agreement with OpenAI (Cerebras Systems, 2026b), and NVIDIA paid US$20 B for a non-exclusive licence to Groq’s inference-only architecture (Groq, 2025; NVIDIA, 2026a). Neither transaction con-cerns training. A second shift has changed what is inferred. Chat com-pletion is one forward pass followed by a token stream; a serving system batches hundreds of independent users to amortise reading model weights (Yu et al., 2022; Kwon et al., 2023). Agentic systems are different in kind [E] (Yao et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2024; Yehudai et al., 2025). A cod-ing agent (Jimenez et al., 2024) or a customer-operations agent (Yao et al., 2024) executes a trajectory : it reasons-often emitting thousands of hidden tokens (Snell et al., 2024;

Each step depends on the previous one; the effective batch of a trajectory is one; latency compounds; the KV cache grows monotonically. These properties collide with how GPU inference has been made efficient. Continuous batching (Yu et al., 2022), paged KV memory (Kwon et al., 2023) and chunked pre-fill (Agrawal et al., 2024) raise throughput by increasing concurrent sequences per weight read; they do nothing for an individual sequential trajectory and in the limit trade it away. The metric governing agent task time-single-stream decode latency-is precisely where HBM GPUs are weak-est relative to SRAM-resident accelerators. The industry has moved decisively toward this reading [D] : Cerebras runs CS-3 as a decode tier behind AWS Trainium3 prefill (Ama-zon Web Services & Cerebras Systems, 2026); NVIDIA’s first Groq-derived product, the Groq 3 LPX rack, is posi-tioned as a decode co-processor for Vera Rubin (NVIDIA, 2026a); SambaNova launched SN50 “for agentic inference” with an Intel Xeon 6 heterogeneous design and was selected by JPMorganChase for on-premises inference (SambaNova Systems, 2026). Roofline and the memory wall [E] . On a roofline (Williams et al., 2009) (Fig. 1b), batch-1–8 decode sits at 1–8 FLOP/byte, far left of any HBM device’s ridge point (≈ 300 FLOP/byte for H100 at FP8). The memory wall (Wulf & McKee, 1995; Gholami et al., 2024)-peak FLOPs growing ∼ 3× faster than DRAM bandwidth over two decades-is therefore the first-order determinant of single-stream decode speed. Three responses exist: raise B (batch-ing, at a latency cost), raise BW (HBM3e →HBM4, or on-die SRAM), or reduce W per step (quantisation, MoE spar-sity (Fedus et al., 2022), speculative decoding (Leviathan et al., 2023)). GPUs pursue all three; SRAM accelerators bet on BW .

Batching, Little’s law and goodput [E] . Little’s law (Lit-tle, 1961) ties in-flight requests N = λW to arrival rate and residence time: higher λ forces larger batches and, via Eq. 1 and queueing, higher per-token latency. The latency– throughput frontier (Fig. 7) is intrinsic. Three consequences: (i) since ni multiplies TPOT and K multi-plies everything, decode dominates for any reasoning agent (ni ≳ 100 ); (ii) the benefit saturates when ni TPOT < τ i-the tool-latency floor is the agentic Amdahl fraction; (iii) because context grows, capacity-tier bandwidth, not only weight bandwidth, sets late-episode TPOT.

H7 is the single result most likely to constitute an original systems contribution: it would show that trajectory-level evaluation changes conclusions that appear stable under token-level benchmarks. Threats to validity : no origi-nal measurements (all figures public and heterogeneous); vendor-stated specifications for products not yet shipping in volume (Groq 3 LPX, SN50, Rubin, CS-4); Eq. 4 treats step counts and token lengths as exogenous, whereas faster inference changes agent behaviour (more sampling, more reflection); strict capability-equivalent configurations may not exist for every platform pair, which is why both con-figurations are reported; public agent environments encode assumptions that may not match enterprise workflows and an agent policy can over-fit a toolchain, so cross-policy validation is required; and measurement boundaries (de-Evaluating Inference Compute for Generative AI: A Framework for Enterprise Workloads 

vice/PDU/wall power; API-path vs in-rack latency) are not interchangeable and must be stated. The minimum exper-iment that converts this framework into an empirical pa-per is small: three OpenAI-compatible endpoints (a GPU provider, an SRAM appliance cloud, and a disaggregated path), families S3–S4, axes A1/A3/A5/A6, paired design with bootstrap intervals, fitting Eq. 4 and testing H1–H3 and H7. 

8. Conclusion 

Agentic AI turns inference from batchable completions into sequential trajectories, moving the hardware bottle-neck from throughput to single-stream decode latency-a quantity governed by weight-tier bandwidth, not peak com-pute. SRAM-resident accelerators exploit this directly, and by 2026 the industry, including the GPU incumbent, has converged on pairing them with HBM-rich prefill engines. Reliability compounds exponentially in trajectory length, so determinism and tail latency are performance variables. The evaluation question is therefore no longer “GPU or specialised?” but which prefill/decode pair, through which channel, at what utilisation-measured as goodput at an agen-tic SLO and cost per successful episode, with paired statis-tics, attested vendor evidence, and break-even conditions on capital ratio, utilisation and adoption lead time. The framework is built so that its placeholders, once populated, yield a reproducible cross-platform study; H7 is the result worth running it for.
